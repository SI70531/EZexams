The ten principles fall into three groups: how people make decisions (1–4), how people interact (5–7), and how the economy as a whole works (8–10). The exam most often asks "which principle is this?" and opportunity-cost calculations.

★★ What economics studies: scarcity (稀少性)

  • scarcity (稀少性): society's resources are limited while wants are unlimited (limited resources, unlimited wants), so it cannot produce everything everyone wants.
  • economics (經濟學): the study of how society manages its scarce resources (later: allocating them through supply = demand equilibrium).
  • Economists study decisions at three levels:
    1. People: what to buy (limited budget), how much to work, save and spend (consumption vs. leisure, enjoying now vs. later).
    2. Firms: how much to produce and how many workers to hire (productivity).
    3. Society: how to divide resources among national defense, consumer goods, environmental protection, etc.

How People Make Decisions · Principles 1–4

★★★ Principle 1: People face trade-offs (取捨): the philosophy of comparison

  • To get something we like, we usually have to give up another thing we also like.
  • Personal examples: a party the night before an exam → less study time; wanting more money to buy things (slave to wants) → longer hours, less leisure; protecting the environment (limited resources) → resources cannot go to urban transit.
  • Social examples:
    • guns and butter (大砲與奶油): the more spent on national defense (guns), the less is left for consumer goods that raise the living standard (butter).
    • Pollution control: a cleaner environment and better health, at the price of lower incomes for owners, workers and customers (protecting the environment raises product costs and lowers worker benefits).
ConceptEnglish definitionIn plain words
efficiency (效率)society gets the most from its scarce resourcesMake the pie bigger (efficiency 效率, "gifted class")

The efficiency vs. equality trade-off (效率 vs 公平的取捨): to be fairer, the government redistributes income from rich to poor (taxes, welfare), but this reduces the incentive to work and produce, so the economic "pie" shrinks (high taxes lower willingness to work).

★★★ Principle 2: The cost of something is what you give up to get it (機會成本)

  • Decisions compare the costs and benefits of each option, and cost is measured as opportunity cost (機會成本): whatever must be given up to obtain something (the value of the next-best alternative given up).
  • The opportunity cost of one year of college = tuition, books, fees + forgone wages (放棄的工資). Room and board do not count, because you would need housing and food anyway.
  • The opportunity cost of going to a movie = the ticket price + the value of the time you spend in the theater.

看解答

Opportunity cost vs. accounting cost (p18 figure)

How to read the figure: both bars have height Revenue (收入).

  • explicit costs (外顯成本): money actually paid out (wages, materials, rent).
  • implicit costs (隱性成本): nothing is paid, but something is given up (the salary the owner's own time could earn, the interest the owner's own funds could earn).
  • The accountant subtracts only explicit costs → accounting profit (會計利潤) = revenue − explicit costs, the larger number.
  • The economist subtracts all opportunity costs (explicit + implicit) → economic profit (經濟利潤) = revenue − explicit − implicit, the smaller number.

★★★ Principle 3: Rational people think at the margin (邊際思考)

  • rational people (理性的人): systematically and purposefully do the best they can to achieve their objectives, given the available opportunities.
  • Decisions are made by weighing a marginal change (邊際變動): a small incremental adjustment to a plan. Rule: act only if marginal benefit MB (邊際利益) > marginal cost MC (邊際成本); the optimum is at MB = MC. Example: at a sushi restaurant, whether to take one more plate depends on the benefit and cost of that plate, not the average.
  • Example 1: people with unlimited-minutes phone plans make long, boring calls because at the margin each minute is free (MC = 0), so they call whenever MB > 0. Similar: empty airline seats are sold cheaply because the marginal cost of carrying one more passenger is very low.
  • Example 2: a manager deciding whether to raise output compares the labor and material cost with the "extra revenue".
  • The water–diamond paradox (水與鑽石的矛盾): water is a necessity yet cheaper than diamonds. Water is plentiful, so the marginal benefit of one more glass is small; diamonds are scarce, so one more has a large marginal benefit. Price reflects marginal value.
In-class Question (p21): sunk cost (沉沒成本)A company has already invested $5 million in developing a new product that isn't finished. After a competing product launches, expected sales fall to $3 million. Finishing development and production costs another $1 million. Should it proceed? What is the most it would pay to finish development?
看解答

The $5M already spent is a sunk cost (沉沒成本): it cannot be recovered whether or not you proceed, so it should not affect the decision. Look only at the margin: spending another $1M (MC) gets $3M (MB), 3 > 1 → do it. The most you would pay is $3 million (any completion cost under 3M is worthwhile).

★★★ Principle 4: People respond to incentives (誘因)

  • incentive (誘因): something that induces a person to act (a reward or a punishment). Rational people compare costs and benefits, so behavior changes when costs or benefits change.
  • Gas prices rise → consumers buy more hybrids and fewer gas-guzzling SUVs. Cigarette tax rises → less teen smoking.
  • The seat-belt problem: after seat belts were promoted, why did driver deaths not clearly fall? Because with a seat belt on, the cost of driving fast is lower → drivers drive faster and more carelessly → more accidents and more pedestrian deaths. Policy must consider indirect incentive effects.
In-class Question (p26)The 1996 US welfare reform law limited many welfare recipients to 2 years of benefits. A. What does this do to the incentive to work? B. How does it show the trade-off between equality and efficiency?
看解答

A: benefits with a time limit → the cost of not working rises → stronger incentive to work, so people find jobs sooner.
B: this raises efficiency (more people work, output grows), but those still jobless after two years may be worse off, so equality falls.

How People Interact · Principles 5–7

★★ Principle 5: Trade can make everyone better off (貿易)

  • Trade is not a contest with one winner and one loser. People can buy a greater variety of goods and services at lower cost.
  • Countries gain from trade and specialization (專業分工): they sell what they produce at better prices abroad (sell high) and buy from abroad what is cheaper than making it themselves (buy low).
  • Slide example "don't put all your eggs in one basket" (p29, United Daily News 2023/9/4): from March to July 2023 Taiwan imported 145.31 million eggs, 88.91 million of them (61%) from Brazil. The opposition criticized that Brazil had been declared a bird-flu area in May yet imports continued. → Trade has benefits, but over-reliance on a single source is risky.

★★★ Principle 6: Markets are usually a good way to organize economic activity (市場)

  • market (市場): a group of buyers and sellers (not necessarily in one place).
  • "Organizing economic activity" = deciding what to produce, how much, who produces, who consumes.
  • market economy (市場經濟): resources are allocated by the decentralized decisions of many firms and households interacting in markets (household decisions vs. firm decisions).
  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (國富論, 1776): every household and firm is led by an invisible hand (看不見的手) to promote overall economic well-being, though that is not their intention; they simply take market prices as given (the price mechanism).
  • Prices are set by the interaction of buyers and sellers and reflect both the value of the good to buyers and the cost of producing it. The invisible hand = prices guide self-interested households and firms to decisions that maximize society's economic well-being.

How the Economy as a Whole Works · Principles 8–10

★★★ Principle 8: A country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce goods and services

  • Living standards differ hugely across countries and eras: rich countries' average income is over 10 times that of poor ones; the US today lives about 8 times better than 100 years ago.
  • The most important determinant is productivity (生產力): the quantity of goods and services produced per unit of labor input.
  • Productivity depends on the equipment, skills and technology available to workers. Other factors (unions, foreign competition) matter much less.
  • Policy implication: raise living standards through education, good tools and access to the best technology.

★★★ Principle 9: Prices rise when the government prints too much money

  • inflation (通貨膨脹): an increase in the overall level of prices in the economy.
  • In the long run, inflation is almost always caused by excessive growth in the quantity of money, which lowers the value of money; the faster the government prints, the higher the inflation rate.
In-class Question (p43): the inflation tax (通貨膨脹稅)During the American Revolution the colonies couldn't raise enough taxes and printed money to fill the gap, called an "inflation tax". Who was "taxed"? Why?
看解答

Those taxed are the holders of money. Printing money raises prices and cuts the purchasing power of cash on hand, so part of their wealth is effectively taken by the government (which buys things with the newly printed money).

★★ Principle 10: In the short run, society faces a trade-off between inflation and unemployment

  • Over a year or two, many policies move inflation and unemployment in opposite directions. Other factors can make the trade-off better or worse, but it never disappears.
  • Process: print money → spending rises → prices rise, firms raise output and hire → inflation up, unemployment down.
  • In the long run, unemployment is unrelated to printing money (only prices rise).

★★ Application: was your decision to go to college right? (Eye on the Benefit and Cost of School)

  • Benefit: enjoying student life now + higher income in the future.
  • Cost (full-time student): tuition, books, other study costs + forgone earnings (放棄的所得).
  • The cost is paid now, while the benefit is spread over a whole working life. For most people the net benefit is large.
  • But not everyone should go to school: Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg dropped out of Harvard; pitcher Clayton Kershaw turned down a Texas A&M scholarship. All three expected the benefit of school < its opportunity cost (their time is too valuable).

Economists play two roles: scientists (explain the world) and policy advisors (improve the world). Scientists use models, the two most important being the circular-flow diagram (循環流向圖) and the production possibilities frontier PPF (生產可能線); policy advisors make normative statements.

★★ The Economist as a Scientist (經濟學家是科學家)

  • Economists play two roles: ① Scientists: try to explain the world ② Policy advisors: try to improve the world.
  • As scientists, economists use the scientific method (科學方法): dispassionately building and testing theories about how the world works (bold hypotheses, careful verification). Economists can rarely run experiments, so they often test with historical events (natural experiments).
  • assumptions (假設): simplify a complex world so it is easier to understand (so the important facts stand out). Example: in studying international trade, assume only two countries and two goods.
  • economic models (經濟模型): highly simplified representations of complex reality. Examples: the tooth model in a dentist's office (Don't forget to floss!), the human-body model in high-school biology, a road map. A model leaves out details so the main point is visible.

  • Two types of decision makers: Firms (廠商) and Households (家計單位).
  • Two markets：Market for goods and services (商品與服務市場)、Market for factors of production (生產要素市場，即投入)。

★★★ PPF and opportunity cost: the slope (斜率)

  • Moving along the PPF = shifting resources from producing one good to another → society faces a trade-off: more of one means less of the other.
  • The slope of the PPF = the opportunity cost of one good in terms of the other. Slope = rise / run = −1,000 / 100 = −10 → the opportunity cost of 1 computer = 10 tons of wheat.
  • Rule of thumb: the (absolute) slope of the PPF is the opportunity cost of the good on the x-axis.
Active Learning 2（p20–21）Which country has the lower opportunity cost of producing cloth?
看解答

Cloth is on the x-axis, so the slope is the opportunity cost of cloth. France: 600 wine / 300 cloth → slope 2 (2 wine per 1 cloth); Britain: 200 wine / 300 cloth → slope 2/3 (2/3 wine per 1 cloth). Britain's PPF is flatter → Britain has the lower opportunity cost of cloth.

★★★ Shape and shifts of the PPF

  • Economic growth (經濟成長): more resources or better technology → more computers, more wheat, or any combination in between → the whole PPF shifts outward.
  • A straight-line PPF: constant opportunity cost (in the example, 1 computer is always = 10 tons of wheat).
  • A bowed-outward PPF: increasing opportunity cost: the more of a good you make, the more of the other good you give up for each extra unit.

Why is it bowed outward? (p24–26 beer vs. mountain bikes)

  • Point A: most workers are brewing beer, even those who are better at making bikes. Moving a few people to mountain bikes costs little beer → the opportunity cost of mountain bikes is low. (A big brewery has many mediocre brewers, even bike specialists.)
  • Point B: most workers are making bikes, and only the best brewers are left. Making more bikes means pulling away top brewers → beer falls sharply → the opportunity cost is high. (With few people left, each is elite, so output falls fast.)
  • Conclusion: when workers have different skills, or resources (e.g., land suited to different uses) have different opportunity costs, the PPF bows outward.

Shifts of the PPF (p28): if technology improves for only one good, the PPF pivots outward along that axis; only if both improve (neutral) does the whole curve shift out in parallel.

In-class Question (p29)Use a PPF to show the trade-off between a "clean environment" and "industrial output". If engineers invent a less polluting way to generate power, how does the PPF change?
看解答

The PPF is bowed out, with industrial output and environmental quality on the two axes. A new power-generation technology allows the same industrial output with a cleaner environment → the PPF shifts outward along the "clean environment" axis (pivots); the maximum industrial output (when the environment is fully sacrificed) is unchanged.

★★★ Where do the gains come from? Absolute advantage (絕對利益)

  • absolute advantage (絕對利益): the ability to produce a good using fewer inputs. It measures the "input" cost.
  • The US needs 10 hours per ton of wheat vs. Japan's 25 → the US has an absolute advantage in wheat; the US needs 100 hours per computer vs. Japan's 125 → the US has an absolute advantage in computers too.
  • The US has an absolute advantage in both! So why does Japan specialize in computers, and why do both gain? → Because each country can specialize in the good with the lowest opportunity cost.
  • Note: Adam Smith held that a country should produce what it has an absolute advantage in and trade for the rest, but that cannot explain trade between advanced and backward countries. David Ricardo (李嘉圖) proposed the theory of comparative advantage to explain it.

★★★ Comparative advantage (比較利益) and opportunity cost

  • Another way to measure cost: opportunity cost. The opportunity cost of 1 computer = the amount of wheat that the labor needed for 1 computer could have produced.
  • comparative advantage (比較利益): the ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost.
  • principle of comparative advantage: each good should be produced by the one with the smaller opportunity cost → Specialize according to comparative advantage。

補充（p29）
  • A country can have an absolute advantage in every good.
  • A country cannot have a comparative advantage in every good (with two goods, if one country has it in one good, the other must have it in the other).
  • The opportunity cost of one good is the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of the other.

At what price do both sides gain from trade?

In the textbook example, the US trades 700 tons of wheat for 110 computers → 1 computer ≈ 6.4 tons of wheat. The US gives up 10 tons to make one computer itself; Japan gives up only 5. The price must lie between the two countries' opportunity costs (5 to 10 tons of wheat) for both to gain.

★★ Comparative Advantage and Trade（總結）

  • The gains from trade come from comparative advantage (differences in opportunity cost).
  • When each country specializes in the good in which it has a comparative advantage: total output rises, the world's economic pie grows, and all countries can gain from trade.
  • The principle of comparative advantage applies to countries and individuals, and economists use it to argue for free trade (自由貿易) between countries.

SOP for computing comparative advantage
  1. Check whether the problem gives "hours per unit" or "units per hour".
  2. Compute each person's opportunity cost of each good: given time → OC(X) = time for X ÷ time for Y; given output → OC(X) = output of Y ÷ output of X.
  3. The one with the lower OC makes that good (comparative advantage); the two people's OCs are reciprocals.
  4. The trading price must fall between the two people's OCs.
Common trap (易錯點)
  • Absolute advantage looks at "fewer inputs"; comparative advantage looks at "lower opportunity cost". The gains from trade depend only on comparative advantage.
  • When the problem gives output, do not compare outputs directly as opportunity costs; convert first.
  • A person can have an absolute advantage in both goods but cannot have a comparative advantage in both.
  • Trade makes the country as a whole better off, but some people at home (workers in industries competing with imports) become worse off.

★★★ Three steps for analyzing changes in equilibrium (hybrid-car example)

  1. Does the event shift the supply curve, the demand curve, or both?
  2. Does the curve shift right or left?
  3. Use the supply–demand diagram to compare the new and old equilibrium and see the change in price and quantity.
  • Example 1: the gasoline price rises. Step 1: it affects demand (gas prices affect willingness to buy hybrids; they do not change the cost of making hybrids, so S does not move). Step 2: hybrids become relatively more attractive → D shifts right. Step 3: both price and quantity rise.
  • Example 2: new technology lowers production cost. Step 1: it affects supply (technology is not a demand factor). Step 2: costs fall, production is more profitable at every price → S shifts right. Step 3: price falls, quantity rises.
  • Example 3: two things happen at once → both curves shift right → Q definitely rises, P is ambiguous: if demand rises more, P rises; if supply rises more, P falls.

Elasticity = "%ΔQ ÷ %ΔP", measuring how sensitive buyers or sellers are to price (or income, or another good's price). Three must-knows: midpoint calculation, elasticity and total revenue, and the applications (wheat, OPEC, drugs).

★★ Opening scenario and the definition of elasticity (彈性)

Scenario: you design websites for local businesses, charging $200 each and selling 12 a month. Your costs rise (including the opportunity cost of your time), so you consider raising the price to $250. The law of demand says you will sell fewer, but how many fewer? Will revenue fall or actually rise? Elasticity answers this.

  • elasticity (彈性): a measure of how much Qd or Qs responds to a change in one of its determinants.
  • price elasticity of demand (需求價格彈性): how much the quantity demanded responds to a change in its own price; roughly the buyers' price sensitivity.
Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price Example: P rises 10%, Q falls 15% → elasticity = 15% / 10% = 1.5

Along a demand curve P and Q move in opposite directions, so the result would be negative; the textbook drops the minus sign and always reports a positive number.

★★★ Computing percentages: why use the midpoint method (中點法)

Website example: point A ($200, 12) and point B ($250, 8). With the ordinary method ((end − start)/start):

  • A → B：%ΔP = (250−200)/200 = 25%，%ΔQ = (8−12)/12 = −33% → 彈性 = 33/25 = 1.32
  • B → A：%ΔP = (200−250)/250 = −20%，%ΔQ = (12−8)/8 = 50% → 彈性 = 50/20 = 2.5
  • The answer depends on direction! So use the midpoint method (中點法): the denominator is the average of the start and end values.
% change = (end value − start value) / midpoint × 100% Elasticity = [(Q₂ − Q₁) / ((Q₂ + Q₁)/2)] ÷ [(P₂ − P₁) / ((P₂ + P₁)/2)] Website: %ΔP = 50/225 = 22.2%; %ΔQ = 4/10 = 40%; elasticity = 40/22.2 = 1.8

★★★ Elasticity along a straight-line demand curve

  • A straight-line demand curve has a constant slope (each $1 increase cuts purchases by 2 units) but non-constant elasticity.
  • Elasticity is about percentages, which depend on the current P and Q: in the upper part Q is tiny, so a small change is a large percentage → elastic (the most elastic point is where consumers buy very little at a very high price); the lower part is the opposite → inelastic.
  • At the midpoint ($15, 30) elasticity = 1, and TR is at its maximum.

★★★ The Price Elasticity of Supply (供給價格彈性)

Price elasticity of supply = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price (again with the midpoint method) Example: P rises 8%, Qs rises 16% → elasticity = 2

Roughly measures sellers' price sensitivity. The five types mirror demand: perfectly inelastic (=0, vertical), inelastic (<1, relatively steep), unit elastic (=1), elastic (>1, relatively flat), perfectly elastic (=∞, horizontal). The flatter the supply curve, the greater the elasticity.

Determinants of the elasticity of supply (供給彈性)

  • The easier it is for sellers to change output, the more elastic the supply: beachfront property is hard to expand → less elastic than the supply of new cars.
  • Longer run is more elastic: in the long run firms can build new factories and new firms can enter.
  • The same supply curve can become less elastic as Q grows (p55): at low output there is spare capacity, so a price rise from $3 to $4 raises Q from 100 to 200 (elastic); near capacity, $12 to $15 raises Q only from 500 to 525 (inelastic).

★★★ Application 1: can good news for farming be bad news for farmers?

  • A new hybrid wheat raises yield per acre by 20% → supply shifts right → P↓, Q↑.
  • Demand for food is inelastic → the price falls sharply and quantity rises only slightly → total revenue falls from $300 to $220, so farmers' total income declines.
  • The paradox of public policy: the government sometimes pays farmers not to farm (cutting supply raises farm income).

★★★ Application 2: why can't OPEC keep oil prices high?

Short run: consumers can't cut oil use right away and other producers can't raise output right away → supply and demand are both inelastic → OPEC's output cut sends prices soaring. Long run: consumers switch to efficient cars and public transit; non-OPEC countries explore more (e.g., shale producers entering the market) → supply and demand both become elastic → prices fall back, and so does OPEC's revenue.

Chapter summary (本章總結)
  • Elasticity measures how much Qd or Qs responds to a determinant. Price elasticity of demand = %ΔQd / %ΔP; < 1 inelastic, > 1 elastic.
  • Inelastic demand → a price rise increases TR; elastic demand → a price rise decreases TR.
  • Goods that are short-run, necessities, broadly defined, or have few close substitutes have more inelastic demand.
  • Price elasticity of supply = %ΔQs / %ΔP, larger in the long run than in the short run.
  • Income elasticity measures the response to income changes; cross-price elasticity measures the response to another good's price change.
  • The supply–demand tools can analyze markets for wheat, oil, drugs and more.
Common trap (易錯點)
  • Elasticity ≠ slope. A straight-line demand curve has a constant slope, but elasticity is high at the top and low at the bottom.
  • Unless the exam says otherwise, always use the midpoint method.
  • "Quantity falls but revenue rises" → inelastic. Revenue rises when price rises ↔ inelastic.
  • Negative income elasticity = inferior good; negative cross elasticity = complements, positive = substitutes.
  • When supply is inelastic, an increase in demand mainly pushes up the "price".

Two tools the government uses to change market outcomes: price controls (價格管制: ceilings and floors) and taxes (課稅). A ceiling below equilibrium → shortage; a floor above equilibrium → surplus. A tax has the same result whoever it is levied on, and the less elastic side bears more of it.

★★ Government policies that alter market outcomes

  • price ceiling (價格上限): a legal maximum price at which a good can be sold. Example: rent control laws.
  • price floor (價格下限): a legal minimum price at which a good can be sold. Example: minimum wage laws.
  • Taxes: the government can require buyers or sellers to pay a fixed amount per unit (this chapter analyzes per-unit taxes only).

★★★ Price ceilings (Example 1: the apartment market and rent control)

  • With no controls the equilibrium rent is $800.
  • A ceiling of $1,000, above equilibrium → not binding (沒有約束力), no effect on the market.
  • A ceiling of $500, below equilibrium → binding (有約束力): $800 becomes illegal, quantity demanded > quantity supplied → shortage.
  • Short run (supply and demand inelastic): demand 400, supply 250 → a small shortage, lower rents (tenants appear to gain).
  • Long run (supply and demand more elastic): demand 450, supply 150 → a bigger shortage. Landlords don't build new apartments or maintain old ones; more people want to rent on their own and move to the city.
  • Inefficient ways of rationing: long waiting lists, preferring tenants without children, racial discrimination, bribing the building manager.

ASK THE EXPERTS, "Rent-control ordinances in New York and San Francisco have a positive effect on the quantity and quality of affordable rental housing" → 95% disagree, 1% agree, 4% uncertain. (A US rent cap limits how much a landlord can raise rents.)

Shortages and Rationing (短缺與配給)

  • When there is a shortage, sellers must ration the good among buyers: long lines, or discrimination based on sellers' biases.
  • These methods are often unfair and inefficient: the good doesn't necessarily go to the buyer who values it most. (A free market rations by price, which is efficient but not necessarily fair.)

★★★ Price floors (Example 2: the unskilled-labor market and the minimum wage)

  • Look only at unskilled labor, because the minimum wage doesn't matter for high-skilled, high-wage workers. The "price" of labor is the wage W. Equilibrium wage: $6.
  • A floor of $5 is below equilibrium → not binding.
  • A floor of $7.25, above equilibrium → binding → labor supply (households) 550 > labor demand (firms) 400 → surplus = unemployment.
  • The minimum wage does not affect high-skilled workers; it affects teenagers: a 10% increase in the minimum wage raises teen unemployment by 1–3% (teen employment falls 1–3%).
  • ASK THE EXPERTS, "Raising the federal minimum wage to $15 by 2020 would noticeably lower employment for low-wage workers" → 34% agree, 29% disagree, 37% uncertain. Upside: helps working poor; downside: causes unemployment.

★★ Evaluating price controls

  • Principle 6, "markets are usually a good way to organize economic activity" → economists usually oppose price ceilings and floors: prices are not arbitrary; they balance supply and demand and coordinate economic activity.
  • Principle 7, "governments can sometimes improve market outcomes" → governments use price controls because market outcomes seem unfair and they want to help the poor; but price controls often hurt the people they are meant to help (rent control means fewer, worse apartments; the minimum wage means teen unemployment).

Chapter summary (本章總結)
  • A price ceiling is a legal maximum price (e.g., rent control); it binds when below equilibrium and causes a shortage.
  • A price floor is a legal minimum price (e.g., the minimum wage); it binds when above equilibrium and causes a surplus; the labor surplus created by a minimum wage is unemployment.
  • Without market failure, the free-market allocation of resources is optimal, and policies that change it often make the economy worse.
  • A tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive, lowering the equilibrium quantity, whoever is taxed.
  • Tax incidence does not depend on who is taxed; it depends on the price elasticities of supply and demand.
Common trap (易錯點)
  • A ceiling binds only if it is "below" equilibrium, and a floor only if "above". The direction is easy to reverse.
  • When a ceiling binds, the quantity traded = the quantity supplied; when a floor binds, the quantity traded = the quantity demanded (take the smaller).
  • Rent control's shortage is larger in the long run than in the short run (elasticity grows).
  • A tax on buyers shifts D down; a tax on sellers shifts S up; the result is the same.
  • The less elastic side bears more. Perfectly inelastic demand → buyers bear everything.

★★ Welfare Economics (福利經濟學)

  • allocation of resources (資源配置): how much of each good is produced, which producers produce it, and which consumers consume it.

★★★ Market Efficiency (市場效率)

Total surplus = CS + PS CS = buyers' value − what buyers pay (buyers' gain from participating in the market) PS = what sellers receive − sellers' cost (sellers' gain from participating) → Total surplus = buyers' value − sellers' cost (the money paid in between cancels out)

Lecture: watch the bargaining scene from Pretty Woman to get a feel for CS and PS.

  • Resource allocation in a market economy is decentralized, determined by the interaction of many self-interested buyers and sellers. Total surplus measures society's well-being and tells us whether the market allocation is efficient. If the market allocation is broadly satisfactory, the government's role should be limited to protecting property rights, national defense, etc.; if not, public policy may improve it.
  • efficiency (效率): an allocation of resources that maximizes total surplus: ① goods are consumed by the buyers who value them most ② produced by the producers with the lowest cost ③ raising or lowering output cannot increase total surplus.
  • Besides efficiency, policymakers also care about equality (公平): whether economic benefits are distributed evenly among members of society.

Evaluating the Market Equilibrium（D：P = 60 − 2Q、S：P = 15 + Q，均衡 P = $30、Q = 15）：

  • Who consumes? Buyers with WTP ≥ $30 buy and those below $30 don't → the free market gives the good to those most willing to buy it.
  • Who produces? Sellers with cost ≤ $30 produce and those above $30 don't → production goes to the lowest-cost producers.
  • Is Q optimal? At Q = 20, the marginal unit costs $35 > the consumer's value of $20 → reducing Q raises total surplus (true for any Q > 15). At Q = 10, value $40 > cost $25 → raising Q raises total surplus (true for any Q < 15). Only the equilibrium Q = 15 maximizes total surplus.

Adam Smith and the Invisible Hand（《國富論》1776）

"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." "Every individual… intends only his own gain, and he is led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention… By pursuing his own interest he frequently promotes that of the society more effectually than when he really intends to promote it." → The invisible hand takes all buyers' and sellers' information into account and guides everyone in the market to the best outcome: economic efficiency. So free markets are usually the best way to organize economic activity.

★★★ Market Efficiency & Market Failure（市場失靈）

  • "The market equilibrium is efficient" rests on two assumptions: ① the market is perfectly competitive ② market outcomes affect only the buyers and sellers in this market. If the assumptions fail, the conclusion may fail.
  • market power (市場力量): a single (or a small group of) buyer or seller can control the market price → the market is not efficient.
  • externalities (外部性): buyers' and sellers' decisions affect people who take no part in the market → from society's viewpoint, the equilibrium is inefficient.

price gouging (哄抬物價): selling necessities after a natural disaster at prices far above normal (generators and camp stoves after losing water, power and gas). Florida and Texas make it illegal. The standard economists' view: it should not be illegal, because it is an efficient response to a change in demand.

  • Normal times: D₀, $20 each, 5 per day.

Chapter summary (本章總結)
  • CS = buyer's willingness to pay − the price actually paid, measuring the buyer's benefit from participating in the market; it is the area below the demand curve and above the price.
  • PS = the amount the seller receives − the cost of production, measuring the seller's benefit; it is the area below the price and above the supply curve.
  • The allocation that maximizes total surplus is called efficient. Policymakers care about both efficiency and equality.
  • The supply–demand equilibrium maximizes total surplus: the market's invisible hand guides buyers and sellers to allocate resources efficiently.
  • With a market failure (market power, externalities), the market cannot allocate resources efficiently.
Common trap (易錯點)
  • CS is "between the demand curve and the price", PS is "between the price and the supply curve"; don't swap them.
  • A buyer whose WTP is below the price doesn't buy → CS = 0, never negative.
  • Total surplus = buyers' value − sellers' cost, independent of price (price only decides how it is split).
  • Efficient does not mean fair. Equilibrium is efficient only given perfect competition and no externalities.
  • A higher price reduces CS in two ways: the original buyers pay more + some drop out.

★★ Review of Chapter 6: the effects of a tax

  • A tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive: the buyers' price rises, the sellers' price falls, and the quantity traded falls.
  • The effect is the same whether the buyer or the seller is taxed.
  • No-tax equilibrium: P = PE, Q = QE. With a per-unit tax $T: buyers pay PB, sellers keep PS (PB − PS = T), quantity QT. Tax revenue = T × QT (the rectangle of wedge times quantity).

★★★ What determines the size of DWL: elasticity

The greater the price elasticity of supply and demand, the larger the DWL of a tax.

  • Inelastic supply: when the tax lowers PS, firms can hardly leave the market → Q falls only a little → small DWL. The more elastic the supply, the easier it is for firms to leave → Q falls more → the larger the DWL (the same tax causes a larger DWL).
  • Inelastic demand: when the tax raises PB, consumers can hardly leave the market → small DWL. The more elastic the demand → the easier it is for buyers to leave (fewer people are willing to buy) → the larger the DWL.

★★ How big should government be? The DWL of the labor income tax

  • Bigger government → more services, but higher taxes are needed → larger DWL. The larger the DWL, the stronger the case for small government.
  • The labor income tax (勞動所得稅) matters especially: it is the government's biggest source of revenue; the US marginal tax rate (邊際稅率) is about 40%.
  • View 1: labor supply is quite inelastic (almost vertical; most people work full time whatever the wage) → the DWL of a labor tax is small.
  • View 2: labor supply is more elastic → the labor tax distorts a lot and the DWL is large: many workers can adjust their hours; in some families a second earner decides whether and how much to work; many older people can choose when to retire; some people work in the underground economy (地下經濟) to evade taxes.

★★★ As the tax grows: DWL and tax revenue

  • When the tax rises, DWL rises faster than the tax: double the tax and the DWL more than doubles (4 times with linear supply and demand); triple the tax and DWL more than triples (9 times). This is because the base and height of the DWL triangle both grow with the tax (DWL ∝ T²).
  • Implication: when tax rates are low, raising them does little harm and cutting them does little good; when tax rates are high, raising them again is very harmful and cutting them is very beneficial.
  • Tax revenue: when the tax is small, raising it increases revenue; when the tax is large, raising it shrinks the market so much that revenue falls (the rectangle at 3T can be smaller than the one at 2T).
  • Laffer curve (拉弗曲線): the relationship between the size of the tax and tax revenue, which first rises and then falls. Economist Arthur Laffer drew it on a napkin in 1974, arguing that US tax rates were already on the right side and that cutting taxes could raise revenue → supply-side economics (供給面經濟學).

★★ Eye on Congress: can Congress decide who pays a tax?

  • Congress requires employers and workers to pay the same Social Security tax (7.65% each in 2015), but because labor demand is much more elastic than labor supply, most of the tax ends up borne by workers.
  • A 20% income tax shifts labor supply from LS to LS + tax: the pre-tax wage rises $1 to $20/hour, the after-tax wage falls $3 to $16/hour, and weekly hours fall from 40 to 35 → workers bear 75%, employers 25%.
  • One thing Congress can do: pass tax laws that don't affect "marginal" decisions. The "Making Work Pay" tax credit (稅額抵減) in the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act ($400 for singles, $800 for couples) is a fixed credit that doesn't affect the choice of hours.
  • With a $30-per-week credit for workers: the marginal tax rate stays at 20%, but workers bear only 68% and employers 32% (employers 35/(35+75)=32%, workers 75/(35+75)=68%) → Congress "bypassed" elasticity.
Chapter summary (本章總結)
  • A tax lowers the welfare of buyers and sellers, and the loss usually exceeds the revenue the government collects.
  • The reduction in total surplus (CS + PS + tax revenue) is called the tax's deadweight loss, DWL.
  • A tax has a DWL because it makes consumers buy less and producers sell less, shrinking the market below the quantity that maximizes total surplus.
  • The price elasticities of supply and demand measure how buyers and sellers respond to price; the greater the elasticity, the larger the DWL.
  • As a tax increases, the DWL grows even faster.
  • As a tax increases, revenue first rises, and eventually falls because the market shrinks.
Common trap (易錯點)
  • DWL = C + E (two triangles), tax revenue = B + D (rectangles). Don't count tax revenue as a loss.
  • DWL has nothing to do with "who is taxed"; it depends on elasticity and the size of the tax.
  • Double the tax: DWL becomes 4 times; revenue rises "by less than double", and may even fall.
  • Perfectly inelastic → no DWL but positive revenue. A tax so high that the market vanishes → no revenue but maximum DWL.
  • Long-run elasticity is greater → long-run DWL is larger and revenue is lower.

★★ Review and the determinants of trade

  • Review of Chapter 3: a country that produces a good at a lower opportunity cost has a comparative advantage in it; countries export the goods in which they have a comparative advantage and gain from trade.
  • Equilibrium without trade: only domestic buyers and sellers; price and quantity are determined by the domestic market, and total benefit = CS + PS.
  • world price (世界價格) PW: the price in the world market. domestic price PD: the domestic price without trade.
  • PD < PW → the country has a comparative advantage → export. PD > PW → no comparative advantage → import.
  • The small economy assumption: a small country is a price taker in the world market, and its behavior doesn't affect PW. Under free trade PW is the only relevant price: sellers won't accept less than PW (they can sell at PW on the world market), and buyers won't pay more than PW.

★★★ Import Quotas (進口配額)

  • import quota (進口配額): a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported.
  • Most effects are the same as a tariff: price rises, imports fall, buyers' welfare falls, sellers' welfare rises.
  • The difference: the tariff revenue E goes to the government; with a quota this area becomes the profit of whoever obtains the quota. The lecture stresses that quotas create profits for foreign producers (who can sell in at a higher price). Only if the government auctions the quota licenses does this area return to the government.

★★ Trade Agreements and the WTO (貿易協定與世界貿易組織)

  • Unilateral approach (單邊途徑): remove trade restrictions on your own. Examples: 19th-century Britain, and more recently Chile and South Korea.
  • Multilateral approach Multilateral approach (多邊途徑): together with other countries一起reduce trade restrictions. Examples: NAFTA, GATT.
  • NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement 北美自由貿易協定): in 1993 lowered trade barriers among the US, Mexico and Canada.
  • GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade 關稅及貿易總協定): many countries keep negotiating to promote free trade. In 2015 the WTO had 162 members, covering over 97% of world trade.
  • Advantages of the multilateral approach: ① it may achieve freer trade than unilateral action (restrictions fall at home and abroad together) ② political benefits: producers are few, organized and politically powerful; a multilateral agreement can win the support of export industries to offset the lobbying of import-competing industries.
Chapter summary (本章總結)
  • World price above the domestic no-trade price → export: PS rises, CS falls, total surplus rises.
  • World price below the domestic price → import: CS rises, PS falls, total surplus rises.
  • A tariff benefits producers and raises government revenue, but consumers' losses exceed those gains.
  • Common arguments for restricting trade: protecting jobs, national security, infant industries, preventing unfair competition, responding to foreign trade restrictions. Some are reasonable in some cases, but economists think free trade is usually the better policy.
Common trap (易錯點)
  • PD < PW → export (not import). If it's "cheaper at home", sell it abroad.
  • Consumers in an exporting country lose; producers in an importing country lose; but in both cases total surplus rises.
  • A tariff's DWL has two pieces: overproduction (D) and under-consumption (F); tariff revenue E is not a loss.
  • Quotas and tariffs have almost the same effects; the difference is who gets area E.
  • The unfair-competition argument: another country's subsidies are good for our country as a whole.

CH1 Ten Principles of Economics（經濟學十大原理）

1-1

What economics studies: scarcity (稀少性)

  • scarcity (稀少性): society's resources are limited while wants are unlimited (limited resources, unlimited wants), so it cannot produce everything everyone wants.
  • economics (經濟學): the study of how society manages its scarce resources (later: allocating them through supply = demand equilibrium).
  • Firms: how much to produce and how many workers to hire (productivity).
1-2

Principle 1: People face trade-offs (取捨): the philosophy of comparison

  • To get something we like, we usually have to give up another thing we also like.
  • Personal examples: a party the night before an exam → less study time; wanting more money to buy things (slave to wants) → longer hours, less leisure; protecting the environment (limited resources) → resources cannot go to urban transit.
  • Pollution control: a cleaner environment and better health, at the price of lower incomes for owners, workers and customers (protecting the environment raises product costs and lowers worker benefits).
1-3

Principle 2: The cost of something is what you give up to get it (機會成本)

  • Decisions compare the costs and benefits of each option, and cost is measured as opportunity cost (機會成本): whatever must be given up to obtain something (the value of the next-best alternative given up).
  • The opportunity cost of one year of college = tuition, books, fees + forgone wages (放棄的工資). Room and board do not count, because you would need housing and food anyway.
  • The opportunity cost of going to a movie = the ticket price + the value of the time you spend in the theater.
1-4

Principle 3: Rational people think at the margin (邊際思考)

  • rational people (理性的人): systematically and purposefully do the best they can to achieve their objectives, given the available opportunities.
  • Decisions are made by weighing a marginal change (邊際變動): a small incremental adjustment to a plan. Rule: act only if marginal benefit MB (邊際利益) > marginal cost MC (邊際成本); the optimum is at MB = MC. Example: at a sushi restaurant, whether to take one more plate depends on the benefit and cost of that plate, not the average.
  • Example 1: people with unlimited-minutes phone plans make long, boring calls because at the margin each minute is free (MC = 0), so they call whenever MB > 0. Similar: empty airline seats are sold cheaply because the marginal cost of carrying one more passenger is very low.
1-5

Principle 4: People respond to incentives (誘因)

  • incentive (誘因): something that induces a person to act (a reward or a punishment). Rational people compare costs and benefits, so behavior changes when costs or benefits change.
  • Gas prices rise → consumers buy more hybrids and fewer gas-guzzling SUVs. Cigarette tax rises → less teen smoking.
  • The seat-belt problem: after seat belts were promoted, why did driver deaths not clearly fall? Because with a seat belt on, the cost of driving fast is lower → drivers drive faster and more carelessly → more accidents and more pedestrian deaths. Policy must consider indirect incentive effects.
1-6

Principle 5: Trade can make everyone better off (貿易)

  • Trade is not a contest with one winner and one loser. People can buy a greater variety of goods and services at lower cost.
  • Countries gain from trade and specialization (專業分工): they sell what they produce at better prices abroad (sell high) and buy from abroad what is cheaper than making it themselves (buy low).
  • Slide example "don't put all your eggs in one basket" (p29, United Daily News 2023/9/4): from March to July 2023 Taiwan imported 145.31 million eggs, 88.91 million of them (61%) from Brazil. The opposition criticized that Brazil had been declared a bird-flu area in May yet imports continued. → Trade has benefits, but over-reliance on a single source is risky.
1-7

Principle 6: Markets are usually a good way to organize economic activity (市場)

  • market (市場): a group of buyers and sellers (not necessarily in one place).
  • "Organizing economic activity" = deciding what to produce, how much, who produces, who consumes.
  • market economy (市場經濟): resources are allocated by the decentralized decisions of many firms and households interacting in markets (household decisions vs. firm decisions).
1-8

Principle 7: Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes (政府)

  • Note it says "sometimes" improves: policies can also be badly designed or distorted by politics.
1-9

Principle 8: A country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce goods and services

  • Living standards differ hugely across countries and eras: rich countries' average income is over 10 times that of poor ones; the US today lives about 8 times better than 100 years ago.
  • The most important determinant is productivity (生產力): the quantity of goods and services produced per unit of labor input.
  • Productivity depends on the equipment, skills and technology available to workers. Other factors (unions, foreign competition) matter much less.
1-10

Principle 9: Prices rise when the government prints too much money

  • inflation (通貨膨脹): an increase in the overall level of prices in the economy.
  • In the long run, inflation is almost always caused by excessive growth in the quantity of money, which lowers the value of money; the faster the government prints, the higher the inflation rate.
1-11

Principle 10: In the short run, society faces a trade-off between inflation and unemployment

  • Over a year or two, many policies move inflation and unemployment in opposite directions. Other factors can make the trade-off better or worse, but it never disappears.
  • Process: print money → spending rises → prices rise, firms raise output and hire → inflation up, unemployment down.
  • In the long run, unemployment is unrelated to printing money (only prices rise).
1-12

Application: was your decision to go to college right? (Eye on the Benefit and Cost of School)

  • Benefit: enjoying student life now + higher income in the future.
  • Cost (full-time student): tuition, books, other study costs + forgone earnings (放棄的所得).
  • The cost is paid now, while the benefit is spread over a whole working life. For most people the net benefit is large.

CH2 Thinking Like an Economist（像經濟學家一樣思考）

2-1

The Economist as a Scientist (經濟學家是科學家)

  • Economists play two roles: ① Scientists: try to explain the world ② Policy advisors: try to improve the world.
  • As scientists, economists use the scientific method (科學方法): dispassionately building and testing theories about how the world works (bold hypotheses, careful verification). Economists can rarely run experiments, so they often test with historical events (natural experiments).
  • assumptions (假設): simplify a complex world so it is easier to understand (so the important facts stand out). Example: in studying international trade, assume only two countries and two goods.
2-2

Model 1: circular-flow diagram (循環流向圖)

  • A visual model of the economy: it shows how money flows through markets between households and firms. To teach engineering students, MIT professor Paul Samuelson (薩繆森) used a picture of "water flowing through a system of pipes" to show how income circulates in the economy (slide p7).
  • Two types of decision makers: Firms (廠商) and Households (家計單位).
  • Two markets：Market for goods and services (商品與服務市場)、Market for factors of production (生產要素市場，即投入)。
2-3

Model 2: production possibilities frontier (生產可能線 PPF)

  • PPF: a graph of the combinations of output the economy can produce given its existing factors of production and technology.
  • Lecture example: two goods (computers, wheat), one resource (labor, in hours), with 50,000 labor hours per month. One computer takes 100 hours; one ton of wheat takes 10 hours.
2-4

PPF and opportunity cost: the slope (斜率)

  • Moving along the PPF = shifting resources from producing one good to another → society faces a trade-off: more of one means less of the other.
  • The slope of the PPF = the opportunity cost of one good in terms of the other. Slope = rise / run = −1,000 / 100 = −10 → the opportunity cost of 1 computer = 10 tons of wheat.
  • Rule of thumb: the (absolute) slope of the PPF is the opportunity cost of the good on the x-axis.
2-5

Shape and shifts of the PPF

  • Economic growth (經濟成長): more resources or better technology → more computers, more wheat, or any combination in between → the whole PPF shifts outward.
  • A straight-line PPF: constant opportunity cost (in the example, 1 computer is always = 10 tons of wheat).
  • A bowed-outward PPF: increasing opportunity cost: the more of a good you make, the more of the other good you give up for each extra unit.

3-4

Where do the gains come from? Absolute advantage (絕對利益)

  • absolute advantage (絕對利益): the ability to produce a good using fewer inputs. It measures the "input" cost.
  • The US needs 10 hours per ton of wheat vs. Japan's 25 → the US has an absolute advantage in wheat; the US needs 100 hours per computer vs. Japan's 125 → the US has an absolute advantage in computers too.

Comparative advantage (比較利益) and opportunity cost

  • Another way to measure cost: opportunity cost. The opportunity cost of 1 computer = the amount of wheat that the labor needed for 1 computer could have produced.
  • comparative advantage (比較利益): the ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost.
  • principle of comparative advantage: each good should be produced by the one with the smaller opportunity cost → Specialize according to comparative advantage。
3-6

  • The gains from trade come from comparative advantage (differences in opportunity cost).
  • When each country specializes in the good in which it has a comparative advantage: total output rises, the world's economic pie grows, and all countries can gain from trade.
  • The principle of comparative advantage applies to countries and individuals, and economists use it to argue for free trade (自由貿易) between countries.

CH4 The Market Forces of Supply and Demand（供給與需求）

4-1

Markets and Competition (市場與競爭)

  • market (市場): a group of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service. Buyers as a group determine demand, and sellers as a group determine supply.
  • competitive market (競爭市場): many buyers and sellers, each with a negligible effect on the market price.
  • perfectly competitive market (完全競爭市場): ① all goods are exactly the same ② buyers and sellers are so numerous that no one can affect the price → everyone is a price taker (價格接受者).
4-2

  • demand schedule (需求表): a table of the relationship between price and quantity demanded. demand curve (需求線): the graph of it, sloping downward to the right.

  • The demand curve shows how price affects quantity demanded "other things equal". Those "other things" are the non-price determinants; when one changes, the whole D curve shifts.

  • quantity supplied (供給量): the amount sellers are willing and able to sell.
  • law of supply (供給法則): other things equal, when price rises the quantity supplied rises; when price falls the quantity supplied falls. The supply curve slopes upward to the right.
  • supply schedule (供給表): at prices of $0–$6 Starbucks supplies 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 cups.

Supply and Demand Together: equilibrium, surplus, shortage

  • equilibrium (均衡): the price reaches the level where quantity supplied = quantity demanded.
  • equilibrium price (均衡價格): the price at which Q supplied = Q demanded (the market-clearing price). In this example $3。

The four combinations of simultaneous shifts and the four rules

  • The law of demand says "price falls → quantity demanded rises"; saying "demand rises" is wrong.
  • Tax software gets cheaper → move along the curve; production costs fall → supply shifts right; professional preparers raise prices → the software's demand shifts right.
  • Buyers expect future price increases → demand now increases; sellers expect future price increases → supply now decreases.

5-1

Opening scenario and the definition of elasticity (彈性)

  • elasticity (彈性): a measure of how much Qd or Qs responds to a change in one of its determinants.
  • price elasticity of demand (需求價格彈性): how much the quantity demanded responds to a change in its own price; roughly the buyers' price sensitivity.

Computing percentages: why use the midpoint method (中點法)

  • A → B：%ΔP = (250−200)/200 = 25%，%ΔQ = (8−12)/12 = −33% → 彈性 = 33/25 = 1.32
  • B → A：%ΔP = (200−250)/250 = −20%，%ΔQ = (12−8)/8 = 50% → 彈性 = 50/20 = 2.5
  • The answer depends on direction! So use the midpoint method (中點法): the denominator is the average of the start and end values.

CH6 Supply, Demand, and Government Policies（供需與政府政策）

6-1

Government policies that alter market outcomes

  • price ceiling (價格上限): a legal maximum price at which a good can be sold. Example: rent control laws.
  • price floor (價格下限): a legal minimum price at which a good can be sold. Example: minimum wage laws.
  • Taxes: the government can require buyers or sellers to pay a fixed amount per unit (this chapter analyzes per-unit taxes only).
6-2

Price ceilings (Example 1: the apartment market and rent control)

  • With no controls the equilibrium rent is $800.
  • A ceiling of $1,000, above equilibrium → not binding (沒有約束力), no effect on the market.
  • A ceiling of $500, below equilibrium → binding (有約束力): $800 becomes illegal, quantity demanded > quantity supplied → shortage.
6-3

Price floors (Example 2: the unskilled-labor market and the minimum wage)

  • Look only at unskilled labor, because the minimum wage doesn't matter for high-skilled, high-wage workers. The "price" of labor is the wage W. Equilibrium wage: $6.
  • A floor of $5 is below equilibrium → not binding.
  • A floor of $7.25, above equilibrium → binding → labor supply (households) 550 > labor demand (firms) 400 → surplus = unemployment.
6-4

Active Learning 1: price controls in the hotel room market

  • A. $90 ceiling: below equilibrium → binding → P falls to $90, demand 120, supply 90 → shortage of 30.
  • B. $90 floor: below equilibrium → not binding → P = $100, Q = 100.
  • C. $120 floor: above equilibrium → binding → P rises to $120, demand 60, supply 120 → surplus of 60.
6-5

Evaluating price controls

  • Principle 6, "markets are usually a good way to organize economic activity" → economists usually oppose price ceilings and floors: prices are not arbitrary; they balance supply and demand and coordinate economic activity.
  • Principle 7, "governments can sometimes improve market outcomes" → governments use price controls because market outcomes seem unfair and they want to help the poor; but price controls often hurt the people they are meant to help (rent control means fewer, worse apartments; the minimum wage means teen unemployment).
  • Other ways to help the disadvantaged: rent subsidies (房租補貼) and wage subsidies (EITC, the earned income tax credit: workers receive a credit equal to a share of earnings; the credit rises with income, levels off at a threshold, then phases out).

CH7 Consumers, Producers, and the Efficiency of Markets（消費者、生產者與市場效率）

7-1

Welfare Economics (福利經濟學)

  • allocation of resources (資源配置): how much of each good is produced, which producers produce it, and which consumers consume it.
  • welfare economics (福利經濟學): the study of how the allocation of resources affects economic well-being. It looks at the benefits buyers and sellers get from market trade and how society can maximize them.
  • The conclusion first: the supply–demand equilibrium of any market maximizes the combined benefits to all buyers and sellers.
7-2

Willingness to Pay (願付價格) and the demand curve

  • willingness to pay (WTP) (願付價格): the maximum amount a buyer will pay for a good, reflecting how much the buyer values it.
  • Example: four buyers' WTP for an iPod: Anthony $250, Chad $175, Flea $300, John $125. At P = $200, Anthony and Flea buy, Qd = 2.
  • The demand curve is a 4-step staircase, one buyer per step; with very many buyers (a competitive market) the steps are many and tiny and look like a smooth curve.
7-3

Consumer Surplus (消費者剩餘)

  • Smooth demand curve (shoes): at P = $30, Q = 15, CS = ½ × 15 × $30 = $225 (triangle area = ½ × base × height). The marginal buyer at Q = 5 is willing to pay $50, so his CS = $20.
  • A higher price reduces CS: if P rises to $40, CS = ½ × 10 × $20 = $100. Two reasons: ① the buyers who stay pay a higher price (rectangle) ② some buyers leave the market (small triangle).
7-4

Producer Surplus (生產者剩餘) and the supply curve

  • cost (成本): the value of everything a seller must give up to produce a good (including the opportunity cost of the seller's time). Cost is the seller's "willingness to sell": produce only if P > cost.
  • Example: three lawn-mowing sellers: Jack $10, Janet $20, Chrissy $35. Supply schedule: P $0–9 → 0; 10–19 → 1; 20–34 → 2; 35 and up → 3.
  • At any Q, the height of the supply curve = the cost of the marginal seller (邊際賣者) (the seller who would leave if the price were slightly lower). It can also be called "marginal cost" (邊際成本).
7-5

Market Efficiency (市場效率)

  • Resource allocation in a market economy is decentralized, determined by the interaction of many self-interested buyers and sellers. Total surplus measures society's well-being and tells us whether the market allocation is efficient. If the market allocation is broadly satisfactory, the government's role should be limited to protecting property rights, national defense, etc.; if not, public policy may improve it.
  • efficiency (效率): an allocation of resources that maximizes total surplus: ① goods are consumed by the buyers who value them most ② produced by the producers with the lowest cost ③ raising or lowering output cannot increase total surplus.
  • Besides efficiency, policymakers also care about equality (公平): whether economic benefits are distributed evenly among members of society.
7-6

Market Efficiency & Market Failure（市場失靈）

  • "The market equilibrium is efficient" rests on two assumptions: ① the market is perfectly competitive ② market outcomes affect only the buyers and sellers in this market. If the assumptions fail, the conclusion may fail.
  • market power (市場力量): a single (or a small group of) buyer or seller can control the market price → the market is not efficient.
  • externalities (外部性): buyers' and sellers' decisions affect people who take no part in the market → from society's viewpoint, the equilibrium is inefficient.

CH8 The Costs of Taxation（課稅的代價）

8-1

Review of Chapter 6: the effects of a tax

  • A tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive: the buyers' price rises, the sellers' price falls, and the quantity traded falls.
  • The effect is the same whether the buyer or the seller is taxed.
  • No-tax equilibrium: P = PE, Q = QE. With a per-unit tax $T: buyers pay PB, sellers keep PS (PB − PS = T), quantity QT. Tax revenue = T × QT (the rectangle of wedge times quantity).
8-2

How a tax affects CS, PS and total surplus (the A–F areas method)

  • deadweight loss (DWL) (無謂損失) = C + E：由market distortionthe reduction in total surplus (caused by a market distortion such as a tax).
  • B + D is merely transferred from buyers and sellers to the government, so total surplus doesn't shrink on that part; C + E is a loss that nobody receives.
  • The source of DWL: the units QE − QT are not sold because of the tax; for these trades the buyer's value exceeds the seller's cost, so they would have benefited both sides. The tax shrinks the market below the quantity that maximizes total surplus.
8-3

What determines the size of DWL: elasticity

  • Inelastic supply: when the tax lowers PS, firms can hardly leave the market → Q falls only a little → small DWL. The more elastic the supply, the easier it is for firms to leave → Q falls more → the larger the DWL (the same tax causes a larger DWL).
  • Inelastic demand: when the tax raises PB, consumers can hardly leave the market → small DWL. The more elastic the demand → the easier it is for buyers to leave (fewer people are willing to buy) → the larger the DWL.
8-4

How big should government be? The DWL of the labor income tax

  • Bigger government → more services, but higher taxes are needed → larger DWL. The larger the DWL, the stronger the case for small government.
  • The labor income tax (勞動所得稅) matters especially: it is the government's biggest source of revenue; the US marginal tax rate (邊際稅率) is about 40%.
  • View 1: labor supply is quite inelastic (almost vertical; most people work full time whatever the wage) → the DWL of a labor tax is small.
8-5

As the tax grows: DWL and tax revenue

  • When the tax rises, DWL rises faster than the tax: double the tax and the DWL more than doubles (4 times with linear supply and demand); triple the tax and DWL more than triples (9 times). This is because the base and height of the DWL triangle both grow with the tax (DWL ∝ T²).
  • Implication: when tax rates are low, raising them does little harm and cutting them does little good; when tax rates are high, raising them again is very harmful and cutting them is very beneficial.
  • Tax revenue: when the tax is small, raising it increases revenue; when the tax is large, raising it shrinks the market so much that revenue falls (the rectangle at 3T can be smaller than the one at 2T).
8-6

Eye on Congress: can Congress decide who pays a tax?

  • Congress requires employers and workers to pay the same Social Security tax (7.65% each in 2015), but because labor demand is much more elastic than labor supply, most of the tax ends up borne by workers.
  • A 20% income tax shifts labor supply from LS to LS + tax: the pre-tax wage rises $1 to $20/hour, the after-tax wage falls $3 to $16/hour, and weekly hours fall from 40 to 35 → workers bear 75%, employers 25%.
  • One thing Congress can do: pass tax laws that don't affect "marginal" decisions. The "Making Work Pay" tax credit (稅額抵減) in the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act ($400 for singles, $800 for couples) is a fixed credit that doesn't affect the choice of hours.

CH9 International Trade（國際貿易）

9-1

Review and the determinants of trade

  • Review of Chapter 3: a country that produces a good at a lower opportunity cost has a comparative advantage in it; countries export the goods in which they have a comparative advantage and gain from trade.
  • Equilibrium without trade: only domestic buyers and sellers; price and quantity are determined by the domestic market, and total benefit = CS + PS.
  • world price (世界價格) PW: the price in the world market. domestic price PD: the domestic price without trade.
9-2

Gainers and losers in an exporting country (the soybean example)

  • No trade: PD = $4, Q = 500. World price PW = $6 → the domestic price becomes $6: domestic consumers demand 300, domestic producers supply 750 → exports 450.
  • No trade: CS = A + B, PS = C, total surplus = A + B + C.
  • 有貿易：CS = A（減少 B）、PS = B + C + D(adds B + D), total surplus = A + B + C + D → gains from trade = D。
9-3

Gainers and losers in an importing country (Active Learning 1: plasma TVs)

  • No trade: PD = $3,000, Q = 400; PW = $1,500 → domestic demand 600, domestic supply 200 → imports 400.
  • No trade: CS = A, PS = B + C, total surplus = A + B + C.
  • With trade: CS = A + B + D, PS = C, total surplus = A + B + C + D → gains from trade = D. B is transferred from producers to consumers.
9-4

Other benefits of trade, and why some people oppose it

  • Other benefits: consumers get greater variety; producers lower costs through economies of scale (規模經濟); more competition reduces domestic firms' market power (total welfare rises); the exchange of ideas speeds the spread of technology worldwide.
  • ASK THE EXPERTS, "Past major trade agreements have benefited most Americans" → 93% agree, 0% disagree, 7% uncertain.
  • So why do so many oppose trade? Losers have more incentive to organize and lobby to restrict trade: the losses are concentrated on a small group and strongly felt; the gains are spread over many people who may not see the benefit of trade (10 million people each gaining $10 vs. 100 people each losing $100,000). In theory the winners could compensate the losers and still be better off, but such compensation rarely happens.
